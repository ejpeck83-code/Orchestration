Reading 1 · Monday · #1 in the reading order

The engine

Star (1999), The Ethnography of Infrastructure

Cite
Star, Susan Leigh. 1999. “The Ethnography of Infrastructure.” American Behavioral Scientist 43 (3): 377-391.
Maps to
The engine. Each mystery is a breakdown that makes a hidden system visible.
Length
15 pages
Get it
doi.org/10.1177/00027649921955326 (SAGE), through a library login. Most libraries can also get it for you by interlibrary loan.

The argument in brief

Star opens with “a call to study boring things”: plugs, standards, forms. Her case is that the parts of modern life people rarely notice do a lot of the shaping.

Infrastructure, for Star, is relational. The same pipe is invisible to the person using it and the whole job of the person maintaining it.

She lists the properties that make something infrastructure. Two of them are your series in miniature: it’s built on an installed base, and it becomes visible upon breakdown. Her method is ethnography: watch the work, read the documents, follow the people who keep a system running.

Mark these

  • The opening call to study boring things. It’s your collection’s mission statement, written by a sociologist.
  • The list of infrastructure properties. Pencil one of your stories next to each property.
  • Every place she calls infrastructure relational. That’s your license to rotate investigators, since each profession sees a different system.

Why it matters for the series

Every story in the collection is a breakdown, and every breakdown shows the reader a piece of the alternate world for a moment. Star gives you the reason the solve and the reveal can be one act: the system shows itself at the instant it fails.

Reading 2 · Tuesday · #3 in the reading order

The thesis

David (1985), Clio and the Economics of QWERTY

Cite
David, Paul A. 1985. “Clio and the Economics of QWERTY.” American Economic Review 75 (2): 332-337.
Maps to
The thesis. Civilization is made of forgotten decisions.
Length
6 pages
Get it
jstor.org/stable/1805621, through a library login, or by interlibrary loan.

The argument in brief

Why you still type on QWERTY. A layout from the early typewriter business beat later rivals and stayed, because everything around it adapted to it.

David names 3 ingredients and calls the mix “QWERTY-nomics.” Technical interrelatedness: the keyboard has to match what typists’ fingers already know. Economies of scale: every new user makes the standard cheaper for everyone else. Quasi-irreversibility of investment: once millions of typists are trained, switching costs too much.

His point for historians is that sometimes the only way to understand why the world works the way it does is to learn how it got that way.

A small event worth knowing

On July 25, 1888, in Cincinnati, Frank McGurrin touch-typed on a QWERTY machine and beat Louis Traub, who was typing on a rival Caligraph. McGurrin took the $500 prize, and the win helped make QWERTY and touch typing the norm. One contest, one typist, and a standard that has lasted more than a century.

Mark these

  • The 3 ingredients. A fork has to carry all 3 to survive until 1962.
  • Each place where a small, early event decides a long outcome.
  • Where David treats the losing keyboard, Dvorak’s, as better. Liebowitz & Margolis (#4) dispute that evidence. Hand their argument to your in-world skeptic.

Why it matters for the series

This is your thesis in the language of economics. The 3 ingredients explain why a city that bet on air and water mains in the 1890s still runs on them in 1973. The hardware, the skills and the money locked together, and the original argument faded from memory.

Birdsill Holly installed the first successful commercial district heating system in Lockport in 1877, and more than 50 were running by 1890.

C2The Niagara Commission rejected every plan, pneumatic ones included High

In 1890-91 the International Niagara Commission, headed by Lord Kelvin, reviewed schemes ranging from pneumatic pressure to ropes, springs and pulleys, plus direct current. It rejected them all, and Westinghouse won the alternating-current contract in late 1893.

How sure: PBS says this almost word for word, and a search on Oct. 5 agreed.

I3Local Niagara stone probably doesn’t swell in concrete Medium

Quarried Niagara Escarpment dolostone isn’t alkali-reactive, so concrete that grows needs imported stone. The classic swelling rock comes from Kingston, Ontario, where the alkali-carbonate reaction was identified in 1957.

How sure: A search on Oct. 5 confirmed the Kingston history and that the escarpment’s Amabel dolostone is a major Ontario concrete aggregate. I couldn’t confirm the non-reactive claim itself.

If it’s wrong: If local stone can swell, you lose the imported-stone clue.

I4Elevations ran on more than one datum Medium corrected

Cold Room (refrigerator)

R1Ethylene ripens everything nearby High

Ripening fruit gives off ethylene, and tiny amounts, well under 1 part per million, set off ripening in nearby fruit. Labs could measure it at those levels by the early 1960s.

How sure: A classic finding, and the 1962 paper is free on PubMed Central.

Check first: Burg & Burg 1962 (PubMed Central, free) pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC549760. Then: Same paper (#31); Burg & Stolwijk 1959 (#33).

If it’s wrong: Your fast-aging mechanism needs replacing.

R2Low oxygen and high carbon dioxide slow ripening High

Kidd and West showed in the 1920s that raising carbon dioxide and lowering oxygen slows ripening. England’s first commercial “gas storage” for apples opened in 1929.

How sure: A search on Oct. 5 agreed on the dates.

Check first: Controlled atmosphere (Wikipedia) en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Controlled_atmosphere. Then: Kidd & West 1930.

If it’s wrong: Your slow-aging mechanism needs replacing.

R3Temperature can do more than I first said Medium corrected

Chemical quality loss speeds up about 2 to 3 times per 10°C. Microbial spoilage near fridge temperatures is more sensitive, often 4 to 10 times per 10°C, so a cold room drifting from 1°C to 8°C could spoil food several times faster.

How sure: The corpus said temperature alone couldn’t change spoilage much. A search on Oct. 5 turned up Q10 values of 2 to 10 for refrigerated foods, which fits Ratkowsky’s growth model.

Check first: Ratkowsky et al. 1982, Journal of Bacteriology doi.org/10.1128/jb.149.1.1-5.1982. Then: Labuza 1984 (#32).

If it’s wrong: If temperature can’t do it, you’re back to ethylene or atmosphere.
