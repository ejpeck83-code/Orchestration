# Originality check

## The verdict

The collection is original. I couldn't find a published work that combines all 5 of your core moves:

1. A linked collection of standalone mysteries.
2. Each built on a different, plausible engineering fork between 1880 and 1960, in the boring layer of daily life.
3. Set in a mature 1962-82 world where the fork is normal and never explained.
4. The reader works out the fork in the same move the investigator solves the case.
5. A rotating cast of working investigators in one shared city, with recurring companies and institutions.

Plenty of books hold 1 or 2 of these. None I found holds 4, let alone 5.

Three of your seeds sit close to famous works, though: the elevator story, the clerk with two impossible databases, and the station that exists only in maintenance records. Readers who know the genre will spot those comparisons. Change those three, or write them knowing the comparison is coming.

## What I searched

- Alternate history, SF, crime, literary fiction, comics, games, film and TV, looking for technology-divergence stories, infrastructure mysteries and "normalized weird" worlds.
- Sidewise Award short-form winners from 2015 to 2025. Recent winners mostly turn on wars, empires, politics and spaceflight. None is an infrastructure mystery.
- Direct probes for your specifics: a world without traffic lights, paternoster fiction, district-heating and pneumatic mysteries. The closest hits were a Granta essay on paternosters and an unrelated thriller that shares the word "Paternoster" in its title.
- Your own phrase, "infrastructural counterfactual." I found no prior use.

I couldn't get at ISFDB's title search from here, so a few obscure short stories could have slipped past me. The big comparisons below are the ones reviewers and agents will raise.

## What's new here

**The fork lives in the plumbing.** Technology-divergence alternate history usually picks spectacle: Babbage engines, Leonardo's machines, zeppelins. Your forks sit in the hydrant and the thermostat, a layer fiction has barely touched.

**The missing object is the clue.** SF has long built worlds without exposition. Heinlein's "the door dilated" (1942) is the famous example, and Jo Walton calls the technique "incluing." Your twist is making an *absence* the clue. That's Sherlock Holmes's dog that didn't bark ("Silver Blaze," 1892), applied to a whole civilization. I found no series that uses it as a rule.

**The solution and the reveal are one act.** Solving the case and learning the world happen in the same move, so every ending pays off twice.

**No-culprit endings are allowed.** Lem did this in single novels. As a rule for a whole series, with engineering failures and feedback processes as legitimate answers, it's rare.

**Your look is the most borrowed part.** "Future technology that has already become public works," scratched and stickered, is close to Stålenhag's signature. The difference you can claim is plausibility: every object in your world could have been built with 1880-1960 engineering. Say that out loud in any pitch.

## If you pitch it

Agents want one classic comparison and one recent one. Two pairs that work:

- *Pavane* plus *Tales from the Loop*: linked stories in a quietly different world, with the public-works look.
- Lord Darcy plus *The Haunting of Tram Car 015*: fair-play investigations where the world's rules are the key.

A line you could build from: *Linked mysteries in the tradition of Pavane and Lord Darcy, set in the weathered public-works America of Tales from the Loop, where every solution is real engineering.*

For shelving, "infrastructure fiction" and the Mundane SF tradition (the 2004 Mundane Manifesto, which ruled out faster-than-light travel and aliens in favor of plausible, near-Earth science) both describe what you're doing.

## Titles

Your working titles are common. "The Intersection," "The Exchange," "The Lock" and "The Number" are all widely used, which makes the stories hard to find in search. Period trade jargon is more distinctive, and it quietly signals the world:

- The Intersection: *Weave Section*
- The Exchange: *Bridged Tap* or *Dead Pair*
- The Lift: *Turnover*
- The Meter: *Dwell Point*
- The Number: *Datum*
- The Hydrant: *Head Pressure*
- The Crossing: *Inversion*
- The Dial: *Set Point*
